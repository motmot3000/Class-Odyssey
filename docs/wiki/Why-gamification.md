# Why gamification?

Gamification means using game-design elements in a context that is not a game
(Deterding, Dixon, Khaled & Nacke, 2011). In a classroom, it adds familiar
mechanics — points, levels, characters, challenges, visible progress — to a
learning activity that remains a learning activity.

It does not teach by itself. Its value is indirect: it can make useful learning
behaviours more frequent, so that the actual work — retrieving, practising,
explaining, correcting — happens more often and for longer.

## The four advantages that matter in class

| Advantage | What it changes in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Visible progress** | Students see where they are and what comes next, instead of waiting for a grade. A level is immediate; a term report is not. |
| **Immediate, low-stakes feedback** | Effort is acknowledged during the lesson. Mistakes can be corrected without the weight of a final mark, which supports practice and persistence. |
| **Ownership** | A character that belongs to the student, with a name, a look and a history, gives them a reason to care about the class story. Customisation turns effort into something personal. |
| **A shared classroom frame** | Rewards and consequences are explicit, visible and applied to everyone. The teacher can recognise cooperation, effort or progress in one gesture, and the class understands what is valued. |

Two secondary benefits are worth naming:

- **Cooperation.** Whole-class rewards and a shared display encourage students
  to help each other rather than only compete.
- **Classroom management.** A console that applies XP, coins or HP loss in one
  click reduces the friction of managing a class activity, especially on a
  projected screen.

## What the research actually says

The evidence is positive **on average**, but conditional and heterogeneous. The
most frequently cited synthesis, Sailer and Homner (2020), reports small to
medium effects: `g = 0.49` on cognitive outcomes, `g = 0.36` on motivational
outcomes and `g = 0.25` on behavioural outcomes. Bai, Hew and Huang (2020) find
`g = 0.50` on learning. Li, Hew and Du (2024) find only `g = 0.26` on intrinsic
motivation: points and badges do not automatically make students want to learn.

Recent meta-analyses sometimes report larger effects, but with very high
heterogeneity — often `I²` above 88%, sometimes close to 99%. That means the
average describes no particular class well. The design, the subject, the age
group and the quality of the underlying activity matter more than the presence
of a game layer.

Three cautions follow:

- **Engagement is not learning.** More clicks, more time in the app or more
  levels do not prove understanding, retention or transfer.
- **A novelty effect is plausible.** The enthusiasm of the first weeks is not a
  durable result.
- **Some mechanics harm.** Permanent public rankings, controlling rewards and
  playful overload can increase anxiety, comparison and disengagement.

The practical question is therefore not "how do we make the lesson fun?" but
"which learning behaviour is missing, and which minimal mechanic supports it?".
A bad exercise stays a bad exercise, even with badges.

For the full synthesis, sources and effect sizes, see the public article
[Gamification in the classroom: what the science says](https://classodyssey.com/gamification-en.html).

## How Class Odyssey applies it

Class Odyssey implements a deliberately small set of mechanics, each tied to a
classroom behaviour:

- **One hero per student.** Identity and ownership; the character persists from
  week to week.
- **XP and levels.** Visible progression. Levelling up restores HP, so progress
  also gives a fresh start.
- **Virtual coins and cosmetics.** Real choice: students decide what to buy and
  how their hero looks. Coins are never bought with real money.
- **HP and sanctions.** HP loss is a visible, reversible consequence applied by
  the teacher. At zero HP the hero is shown as eliminated; the student, the
  data and the inventory are never deleted. This is a classroom signal, not an
  automatic disciplinary system.
- **Whole-class rewards.** A deliberate toggle for collective recognition,
  supporting cooperation.
- **A protected teacher console.** Fast, explicit feedback during the lesson,
  with a thirty-minute unlock and no separate student accounts.

The app does not grade schoolwork and does not decide who deserves a reward.
The teacher does. The platform only makes the decision visible and quick.

## Fair-use principles

1. **Say what is rewarded.** Effort, progress, help, participation — not
   obedience or speed alone.
2. **Keep sanctions predictable and proportionate.** Announce them, apply them
   consistently, and never use them to humiliate.
3. **Compare students with themselves.** The projected board shows every hero;
   avoid turning it into a public ranking.
4. **Offer a way to participate without the game.** No student should be
   penalised for opting out of a cosmetic system.
5. **Measure what matters.** Look at participation, persistence and work
   quality — not at time spent in the app.
